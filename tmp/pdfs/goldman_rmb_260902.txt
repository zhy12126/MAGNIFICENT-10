exploring a range of potential responses, including trade barriers and pressure

Why Beijing may favor gradual RMB appreciation 
 
 
As China’s trade surplus grows steadily larger – with the goods surplus now 6% of GDP 
and more than 1% of global GDP – anxiety among foreign policymakers about the 
impact on their manufacturing sectors is growing. The United States responded with 
broad tariffs, while other countries and regions (e.g. the EU) have been more selective 
and restrained. Analysts have also publicly debated a range of proposals for alleviating 
global imbalances. US Treasury Secretary Bessent has described China’s trade surplus as 
“unsustainable” and recently called for other countries to “examine their terms of trade 
with China”. 
As we have noted repeatedly in the past, China’s currency is substantially undervalued, 
contributing to its export success. This has been one argument given to justify tariff 
protection against Chinese goods. It also underpins proposals that other economies 
pressure China to allow the renminbi to appreciate more quickly as a way of reducing 
current account imbalances. By contrast, senior IMF officials recently downplayed the 
role of FX, arguing that structural adjustment and US fiscal consolidation is a preferable 
adjustment route. Our own view is that currency adjustment is necessary but not 
sufficient – it would need to be combined with fiscal expansion aimed at boosting 
China’s demand in order to bring China’s own economy back to balance, and with fiscal 
restraint in large deficit economies. 
However, experienced forecasters know that it is important to distinguish the question 
of what policymakers should do from what they are likely to do. China’s stated 
macroeconomic goals and its policy outcomes suggest that rebalancing – either global or 
domestic – does not appear to be high on Beijing’s agenda. In what follows, we attempt 
to take a look at the situation from Beijing’s perspective. 
In our view, China’s policymakers effectively seek to further increase China’s share of 
global manufacturing and move up the technology ladder while becoming less 
dependent on foreign supply. (Aware of foreign concerns about rising surpluses, China’s 
policymakers have claimed they “never deliberately pursued a trade surplus”1, but with 
goals of further strengthening across many manufacturing industries and “self-reliance” 
across a range of critical inputs, it is difficult to see how the math could work out 
otherwise.) Strength in foreign demand is generally accompanied by restraint in 
domestic China macro policy, as has been the case recently (Exhibit 1). Put differently, 
the revealed preference of policymakers appears to be to leverage foreign demand to 
the extent possible to drive China’s growth.

Another policy goal in Beijing – less critical than technology leadership, but nonetheless 
important and related to the “self-reliance” drive – is internationalization of the RMB. 
Success in this endeavor would reduce vulnerability to American sanctions in a world 
where the US dollar remains dominant. Policymakers appear to see a window of 
opportunity to “gain share” in an international currency sense, and have stepped up 
efforts in this regard over the past year in several respects, including bond market 
internationalization. Still another target, unimportant economically but of symbolic 
value to Beijing, would be for China’s economy to exceed the US in size on a market value 
basis.2 
A policy which Beijing might assess could achieve all these goals simultaneously is 
gradual RMB appreciation against the USD. 
From the perspective of continued global manufacturing market share gains, 1.
nominal appreciation against the USD might be seen by trading partners as a token 
of accommodation to foreign concerns, and reduce the likelihood of tariff pushback. 
From the perspective of RMB internationalization, appreciation – if market 2.
participants expect it to continue – helps offset the negative rate differential vs the 
US and would make RMB-denominated fixed income assets more attractive to hold. 
Chinese government bond yields have slid lower for almost a decade, with the 5-year 
CGB yield now about 1.4% and the 10-year 1.7%. Meanwhile, global yields have 
risen sharply from Covid-era lows, with the US 10-year Treasury yield more than 
300bp higher than China’s (Exhibit 2). 
From the perspective of GDP at current prices and market exchange rates, RMB 3.
appreciation could help China gain on the US in relative terms, even as its nominal 
GDP growth slows.3 Exhibit 3 shows that after a period of strong outperformance 
from 2005 to 2020, China’s “catch-up” to US market-value GDP reversed from 2021 
to 2025 as its nominal growth slowed and the renminbi weakened against the dollar.

A challenge for Beijing is that rapid RMB appreciation helps the second and third goals, 
but potentially undermines the first, as it would eventually cut into China’s international 
competitiveness, slowing exports and real GDP growth. But a moderate pace of 
appreciation – perhaps 3-5% a year against the dollar – would not impede ongoing 
market share gains, in our view. 
Economists typically think about the real trade-weighted exchange rate (TWI) as the 
most relevant in terms of international competitiveness, as it encompasses the range of 
trading partners as well as differences in inflation across countries. If gradual enough, 
RMB appreciation against the USD (i.e. declining USDCNY) need not erode 
competitiveness as measured by the real TWI, for two reasons. 
The USD is modestly overvalued by our assessment (with most of this overvaluation 1.
against the RMB and other Asian currencies, particularly north Asia). Our global FX 
forecasts imply roughly a 1% annual depreciation in the broad USD TWI over the 
next several years. If the USD is on a broad depreciation trend, the RMB can 
appreciate at the same pace against the USD without appreciating against other 
currencies (on average). 
Inflation in China has been lower than in most other countries in recent years. Put 2.
differently, China runs a negative “inflation differential” with the rest of the world. 
This gap has been particularly stark during the housing bust, with much of the world 
experiencing an inflationary surge but China in lowflation (CPI) or deflation (PPI) 
during most of this period. This resulted in a sharp real depreciation of the RMB 
against its trading partners from 2022-25 (Exhibit 5) – particularly on a PPI basis, 
which is arguably more relevant from a trade perspective. While this degree of

The above points suggest that the RMB could appreciate 2-3% a year against the USD 
without any appreciation in the real CNY TWI for several years into the future. And of 
course, it could appreciate modestly faster than that – even say 5% a year – and still 
remain meaningfully ‘cheap’ to fair value five years from now. Roughly, if a 5% annual 
appreciation against the USD means a 2.5% annual appreciation in the real RMB TWI, 
and the RMB is 20% undervalued today, then China would have only halved its 
undervaluation after four years. A strong case can be made that the undervaluation is 
larger than 20%, and it’s also possible that the inflation differentials and broad USD 
moves could be bigger than we assume here. In other words, policymakers may be able 
to have their cake and eat it too: 
Appreciation of 3-5% per year against the USD would have only marginal impacts on n
the competitiveness of Chinese firms in the next few years. 
If markets came to expect such appreciation, this would offset China’s lower rates on n
government bonds and related fixed income securities, probably contributing to 
increased international holdings of RMB assets. 
Given sluggish nominal GDP growth in China, currency appreciation could help close n
the gap with US GDP on a market value (dollar) basis. 
This pace of appreciation is broadly in line with forward pricing over the coming year, n
making speculative positions less attractive in either direction and simplifying the 
problem of currency management from the authorities’ (PBOC/SAFE) perspective. 
An implicit assumption in the discussion so far is that Beijing has a significant degree of 
control over the exchange rate. We strongly believe this to be the case – that in broad 
terms the CNY is at least as much a policy variable as a market outcome. While 
movement within the trading band is largely market-determined, the broader path is 
policy-determined. This reflects a combination of both explicit signaling (daily CNY fix), 
other central bank communication, large-scale resources to intervene and willingness to 
do so, and a longstanding capital control regime that attempts to route flows through 
authorized channels (e.g. the Stock/Bond Connect schemes).

From the perspective of China’s household welfare and global imbalances, a better 
policy option would be more rapid appreciation coupled with greater support to 
domestic consumption. To the extent that currency appreciation slows headline GDP 
growth and introduces more deflationary pressures, it would incentivize Chinese 
policymakers to implement demand-boosting reflationary policies, which is another 
necessary condition for restoring economic balances for China. China might hold or gain 
global manufacturing share even in this scenario – for example, in the 2005-15 period, 
China’s share of global manufacturing grew rapidly even as its trade-weighted exchange 
rate appreciated, reflecting its strong competitiveness and the tailwind from WTO 
admission (Exhibit 6). Collectively, these actions would increase households’ purchasing 
power, and reduce global imbalances and trade tensions. Despite some calls by Chinese 
academics and former policymakers to support households, and the rollout of a 
five-year plan to improve consumption, Beijing appears to be moving very cautiously in 
terms of implementation. While the RMB appreciated fairly quickly in late 2025 and 
early this year (Exhibit 7), some of this may have reflected efforts to manage the 
relationship with the US, and recent signals from the daily fixing suggest Beijing would 
prefer to slow the pace going forward (Exhibit 8). 
 
Therefore, we continue to expect modest, low-to-mid-single-digit appreciation of the 
RMB in the coming years. Shocks that could change the path include a re-ignition of 
large-scale tariff war, or a major global economic downturn, either of which would take 
away the export impulse to China. Absent those, we expect USDCNY to reach about 6 at 
end-2028 and 5.50 at end-2031. Strength in the renminbi should also be supportive of 
other Asian currencies; correlations between CNY and other Asian currencies have risen 
in recent years. 
Key near-term events for the bilateral exchange rate are likely to include the US 
employment report and September Fed meeting, but most importantly the scheduled 
visit of President Xi to Washington to meet with President Trump on September 24.
