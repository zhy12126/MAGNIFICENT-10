least 1pp below that of trading partners over the next few years. 
 
The above points suggest that the RMB could appreciate 2-3% a year against the USD 
without any appreciation in the real CNY TWI for several years into the future. And of 
course, it could appreciate modestly faster than that – even say 5% a year – and still 
remain meaningfully ‘cheap’ to fair value five years from now. Roughly, if a 5% annual 
appreciation against the USD means a 2.5% annual appreciation in the real RMB TWI, 
and the RMB is 20% undervalued today, then China would have only halved its 
undervaluation after four years. A strong case can be made that the undervaluation is 
larger than 20%, and it’s also possible that the inflation differentials and broad USD 
moves could be bigger than we assume here. In other words, policymakers may be able 
to have their cake and eat it too: 
Appreciation of 3-5% per year against the USD would have only marginal impacts on n
the competitiveness of Chinese firms in the next few years. 
If markets came to expect such appreciation, this would offset China’s lower rates on n
government bonds and related fixed income securities, probably contributing to 
increased international holdings of RMB assets. 
Given sluggish nominal GDP growth in China, currency appreciation could help close n
the gap with US GDP on a market value (dollar) basis. 
This pace of appreciation is broadly in line with forward pricing over the coming year, n
making speculative positions less attractive in either direction and simplifying the 
problem of currency management from the authorities’ (PBOC/SAFE) perspective. 
An implicit assumption in the discussion so far is that Beijing has a significant degree of 
control over the exchange rate. We strongly believe this to be the case – that in broad 
terms the CNY is at least as much a policy variable as a market outcome. While 
movement within the trading band is largely market-determined, the broader path is 
policy-determined. This reflects a combination of both explicit signaling (daily CNY fix), 
other central bank communication, large-scale resources to intervene and willingness to 
do so, and a longstanding capital control regime that attempts to route flows through 
authorized channels (e.g. the Stock/Bond Connect schemes).
