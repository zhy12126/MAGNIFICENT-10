 
Another policy goal in Beijing – less critical than technology leadership, but nonetheless 
important and related to the “self-reliance” drive – is internationalization of the RMB. 
Success in this endeavor would reduce vulnerability to American sanctions in a world 
where the US dollar remains dominant. Policymakers appear to see a window of 
opportunity to “gain share” in an international currency sense, and have stepped up 
efforts in this regard over the past year in several respects, including bond market 
internationalization. Still another target, unimportant economically but of symbolic 
value to Beijing, would be for China’s economy to exceed the US in size on a market value 
basis.2 
A policy which Beijing might assess could achieve all these goals simultaneously is 
gradual RMB appreciation against the USD. 
From the perspective of continued global manufacturing market share gains, 1.
nominal appreciation against the USD might be seen by trading partners as a token 
of accommodation to foreign concerns, and reduce the likelihood of tariff pushback. 
From the perspective of RMB internationalization, appreciation – if market 2.
participants expect it to continue – helps offset the negative rate differential vs the 
US and would make RMB-denominated fixed income assets more attractive to hold. 
Chinese government bond yields have slid lower for almost a decade, with the 5-year 
CGB yield now about 1.4% and the 10-year 1.7%. Meanwhile, global yields have 
risen sharply from Covid-era lows, with the US 10-year Treasury yield more than 
300bp higher than China’s (Exhibit 2). 
From the perspective of GDP at current prices and market exchange rates, RMB 3.
appreciation could help China gain on the US in relative terms, even as its nominal 
GDP growth slows.3 Exhibit 3 shows that after a period of strong outperformance 
from 2005 to 2020, China’s “catch-up” to US market-value GDP reversed from 2021 
to 2025 as its nominal growth slowed and the renminbi weakened against the dollar.
